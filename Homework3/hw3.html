Homework 3 · Random walks and limit theorems

n systems, m attackers

Every attacker tries to penetrate the systems one after the other and collects a score at each step, so the penetration process is a random walk. On this page I simulate many of these walks with two models for the score, study how the total, the average and the normalized score behave as the number of systems grows, and check the results against the Law of Large Numbers and the Central Limit Theorem.

Part I

The model

Before simulating anything, I define the process precisely and compute the quantities that the simulations are supposed to reproduce.

1.1

Systems, attackers and scores

There are n systems, for example the servers of a network, and m attackers. Each attacker tries to penetrate the systems one after the other, and the attempt on the i-th system gives a score Xi. The penetration score after k systems is the partial sum

Sk = X1 + X2 + … + Xk,   k = 1, …, n

so every attacker produces a trajectory k → Sk, which is a random walk whose steps are the increments Xi.

The increments are assumed to be independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.): all systems are equally hard to penetrate, the outcome on one system does not depend on the others, and all attackers have the same skill and act independently. The m attackers are therefore m independent copies of the same random walk, and their scores after n systems are a sample of size m from the distribution of Sn.

1.2

Two models for the score

Bernoulli model. Xi = 1 if the i-th system is penetrated and 0 otherwise, with P(Xi = 1) = p. Here Sn simply counts the penetrated systems, so it has a Binomial distribution and can only grow.

Rademacher model. Xi = +1 for a successful attempt and −1 for a failed one (for example an attempt that is detected and costs points), each with probability ½. The steps have mean zero, so the walk is symmetric: it has no tendency to go up or down. This is the case studied by the Central Limit Theorem in its simplest form.

Writing B for the number of successes, the Rademacher score is Sn = B − (n − B) = 2B − n, so it always has the same parity as n: after 10 systems the score can only be even.

Bernoulli Rademacher
Values of Xi {0, 1} {−1, +1}
Mean μ = E[Xi] p 0
Variance σ² p(1 − p) 1
Distribution of Sn Bin(n, p) 2·Bin(n, ½) − n
Possible values of Sn 0, 1, …, n −n, −n + 2, …, n

The variance of a single step is E[X²] − μ²: for Bernoulli p − p² = p(1 − p), for Rademacher 1 − 0 = 1.

1.3

Three normalizations

By linearity of the expectation E[Sn] = nμ, and since the increments are independent their variances add up: Var(Sn) = nσ². Dividing Sn by a constant c divides the mean by c and the variance by c², which gives the three quantities of the homework:

Sn = ∑ Xi    Sn/n = (1/n) ∑ Xi    Sn/√n = (1/√n) ∑ Xi

The variance grows linearly for the total score, goes to zero for the average score and stays constant only for the normalized score. The simulations below check these formulas and show what they imply for the shape of the distributions.

1.4

Simulation

Each attacker is simulated step by step with JavaScript's random number generator: a Bernoulli step is 1 when a uniform random number in [0, 1) is smaller than p, and a Rademacher step is +1 or −1 with probability ½. For the Bernoulli model I use p = 0.3.

Every attacker walks through n = 2,000 systems. I store the score at the checkpoints n = 1, 2, 3, 5, 7, 10, …, 2,000 for all attackers, and the complete path for the first 30. In this way all the distributions at different values of n come from the same trajectories, exactly as if I looked at the same attackers at different times.

Choice of m. A few trajectories are enough to see how a walk behaves, but the distributions of Part III are estimated from the m scores, so m sets their precision. A probability P estimated from m attackers has standard error √(P(1 − P)/m) ≤ 1/(2√m), which is with the current m = . The value of m can be changed in the bar at the start of Part II.

2.1

Total score Sk

In the Bernoulli model every path is a staircase that can only go up: each penetrated system adds one point. The paths follow the line kp, and the spread around it grows like √k. Since √k grows more slowly than k, the distance from the mean keeps increasing in absolute terms but becomes small compared with the score itself.

The Rademacher paths have no direction: they oscillate around zero, cross it many times, and spread out in both directions like ±2√k. After n = 2,000 systems, of all Bernoulli attackers and of all Rademacher attackers are inside the dashed band (95% expected).

Distributions as n grows

Here I take the scores of all m attackers at n = 10, 100 and 1,000 systems and look at their distributions. In each row the three histograms share the same horizontal axis, so that changes in position and width are directly visible. 3.1

Total score Sn

In the Bernoulli model the distribution moves to the right with mean np and widens with standard deviation √(np(1 − p)). Its width relative to the mean, √((1 − p)/(np)), decreases: from at n = 10 to at n = 1,000. In the Rademacher model the distribution stays centred at zero and only widens, with standard deviation √n. In both cases the variance of the total score grows linearly with n, as the table confirms.

3.2

Average score Sn/n and the Law of Large Numbers

On the scale [0, 1] or [−1, 1], the distribution of the average score shrinks around its mean as n grows. The weak Law of Large Numbers says that the average converges in probability to the mean of a single step:

for every ε > 0:   P(|Sn/n − μ| > ε) → 0  as n → ∞

For i.i.d. steps with finite variance this follows from Chebyshev's inequality, P(|Sn/n − μ| > ε) ≤ Var(Sn/n)/ε² = σ²/(nε²), which goes to zero because Var(Sn/n) = σ²/n does.

3.3

Normalized total score Sn/√n

With this normalization the width of the distribution no longer depends on n. In the Rademacher model the three histograms are centred at zero with the same spread, and only their shape changes: with 10 systems there are just 11 possible values, with 1,000 systems the histogram is a smooth bell.

In the Bernoulli model the histograms keep the same width √(p(1 − p)) but move to the right with mean √n p, from at n = 10 to at n = 1,000. The distribution has no limit: for every fixed value c, P(Sn/√n ≤ c) → 0. This is why the zero-mean Rademacher model is needed to study Sn/√n; for the Bernoulli model the score would first have to be centred, as in (Sn − np)/√(np(1 − p)).

3.4

The figures show the sample variance of the three quantities over the m attackers at every checkpoint (dots), together with the theoretical values nσ², σ²/n and σ² (lines). On logarithmic axes they are straight lines with slopes 1, −1 and 0.

The three normalizations therefore answer three different questions. Sn describes the absolute score, whose uncertainty grows without limit; Sn/n describes the score per system, whose uncertainty disappears (LLN); Sn/√n is the scale where the fluctuations stay of the same size and their shape can be studied (CLT).

3.5

Central Limit Theorem in the Rademacher case

For i.i.d. steps with mean μ and finite variance σ², the Central Limit Theorem states that (Sn − nμ)/(σ√n) converges in distribution to a standard normal. In the Rademacher model μ = 0 and σ = 1, so this is exactly the normalized score:

P(Sn/√n ≤ x) → Φ(x)  for every x,   Φ(x) = ∫−∞x e−t²/2/√(2π) dt

The histograms are drawn as densities (bar area = fraction of attackers) on top of the N(0, 1) density. Already at n = 10 the bars follow the bell, even though the score takes only 11 values; at n = 1,000 the agreement is very close.

Part IV

Interpretation: systems and attackers

The last step is to translate the results back into the language of the problem. The two parameters play very different roles: n is the length of each walk, while m is the number of walks.

4.1

Effect of the system size n

Bernoulli. The expected number of penetrated systems np grows linearly with the size of the network, and so does the damage an attacker can do. The uncertainty grows only like √n, so the share of penetrated systems becomes more and more predictable: in a large network almost every attacker penetrates a fraction close to p of the systems. Rademacher. With a symmetric score nobody is favoured: the scores are spread symmetrically around zero, and the interval containing 95% of the attackers widens like ±1.96√n, while the same interval for the average score shrinks around zero. A larger system therefore allows larger gains and losses in absolute terms, but they become negligible compared with the number of systems.

4.2

Effect of the number of attackers m

The number of attackers does not change the distribution of the score of any single attacker: every histogram of Part III would have the same shape with more attackers, only less noisy. What m changes is the aggregate picture.

Total over all attackers. The sum T = Sn(1) + … + Sn(m) is a sum of mn independent steps, so it behaves like a single walk of length mn: mean mnμ and variance mnσ². The aggregate penetration score therefore grows linearly with the number of attackers in the Bernoulli model, while in the Rademacher model it has no drift and fluctuates on the scale √(mn). In the simulation T/(mn) = for Bernoulli (against p = 0.3), and T/√(mn) = for Rademacher, a single draw from a distribution close to N(0, 1).

The simulations reproduce the three behaviours predicted in 1.3. The total score spreads out, the average score concentrates, and the normalized score keeps a stable spread whose shape, in the symmetric case, becomes normal. The Law of Large Numbers and the Central Limit Theorem describe the same sum at two different scales: dividing by n shows where the process is going, dividing by √n shows how it fluctuates around it.

For the problem of systems and attackers, a larger n makes the behaviour of each attacker more predictable in relative terms, while a larger m does not change any single attacker but increases the aggregate score linearly in the Bernoulli model and its fluctuations like √m. In the Bernoulli model the defender can plan on a share p of compromised systems per attacker; in the symmetric model the net outcome is a matter of chance, with typical size √n.

Exercise 1

Welford's recurrence formulas

For data x1, x2, … let Mk be the mean and Sk = ∑i≤k (xi − Mk)² the sum of squares of the first k values. The goal is to update both with one new value at a time, without storing the data.

1.1

Knuth's recurrence for the mean

Since kMk = (k − 1)Mk−1 + xk, dividing by k:

Mk = Mk−1 + (xk − Mk−1)/k,   M1 = x1

The new mean is the old one corrected by a fraction 1/k of the deviation of the new value. The same idea is used below for Sk.

useful identity:
k(Mk − Mk−1) = xk − Mk−1
1.2

Recurrence for the sum of squares

Write Sk = ∑ xi² − kMk² and take the difference between two consecutive steps. Using (k − 1)Mk−1 = kMk − xk and the identity of 1.1:

Sk − Sk−1 = xk² − kMk² + (k − 1)Mk−1²
= xk² − kMk² + Mk−1(kMk − xk)
= xk(xk − Mk−1) − Mk · k(Mk − Mk−1)
= xk(xk − Mk−1) − Mk(xk − Mk−1)

which gives Welford's recurrence:

Sk = Sk−1 + (xk − Mk−1)(xk − Mk),   S1 = 0
1.3

Recurrence for the variance

The sample variance is sk² = Sk/(k − 1). Substituting Sk = (k − 1)sk² in 1.2 and dividing by k − 1:

sk² = sk−1² + [(xk − Mk−1)(xk − Mk) − sk−1²] / (k − 1),   k ≥ 2

With divisor k (population variance σk² = Sk/k) the same steps give σk² = σk−1² + [(xk − Mk−1)(xk − Mk) − σk−1²]/k.

1.4

Code

In practice it is enough to update M and S, and divide by k − 1 only at the end. The core is three lines: the deviation from the old mean, Knuth's update of the mean and Welford's update of S.

Unlike the textbook formula ∑ xi² − kMk², it never subtracts two large, almost equal numbers, so it does not lose precision when the values are large compared with their spread. 

function welford(xs) {
  let mean = 0,
    ss = 0,
    k = 0;
  for (const x of xs) {
    k++;
    const d = x - mean;
    mean += d / k;
    ss += d * (x - mean);
  }
  return { n: k, mean, ss, variance: k > 1 ? ss / (k - 1) : 0 };
}

2.3

Conclusions

Comparison with μ and σ². For every n, even n = 5, and for each of the three distributions, the average of the sampling means is close to μ = and the average of the sampling variances is close to σ² = . This is what unbiasedness means: E[x̄] = μ and E[s²] = σ². The divisor n − 1 is needed: with n the average would be σ²(n − 1)/n, i.e. for n = 5.

Why it is useful. In practice only one sample is available and μ, σ² are unknown. Unbiasedness guarantees that x̄ and s² have no systematic error, so they can be used as estimates of the population parameters, and s² gives the standard error s/√n of x̄, on which confidence intervals and hypothesis tests are built.

Effect of n. The averages do not depend on n, but the spread does: the standard deviation of the sampling means follows σ/√n, since Var(x̄) = σ²/n, and for the sampling variances

Var(s²) = [μ4 − σ4(n − 3)/(n − 1)] / n,   μ4 = E[(X − μ)4]

so their standard deviation also decreases like 1/√n: in the simulation from at n = 5 to at n = 1,000. With more systems per hacker, every single hacker's estimates get closer to μ and σ².

Effect of m. The number of hackers does not change these standard deviations, which depend only on n. A larger m only makes the averages over the hackers more precise: the average of the sampling means has standard error σ/√(mn).
